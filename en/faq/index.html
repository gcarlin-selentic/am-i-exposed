Frequently asked questions

The things people want to know before typing their email into a box they have never seen before. If yours is not here, write to us and we will add it.

Do you store my email when I check it?

No. The address is used to run the lookup and then discarded. It is not written to any database, not added to any mailing list, and never travels inside the page address, which is the part that would end up in the server's access logs.

What is kept, briefly, is your IP address, for one purpose only: to stop anyone using the search box to work through thousands of other people's addresses automatically. We would rather say so than pretend it does not exist.

How do you check my password without knowing it?

Your browser works out a fingerprint of the password and sends only the first five characters of it. The service returns every leaked fingerprint that starts the same way, usually several hundred, and the final comparison happens inside your browser. The password itself never leaves your device.

Put another way: whoever receives the query knows somebody asked about a group of passwords, but cannot tell which one. The technique is called k-anonymity, and there is a fuller explanation in how to know if your password was leaked.

Where does the breach data come from?

From Have I Been Pwned, the public breach registry that security researcher Troy Hunt has maintained since 2013. It is the same source used by news outlets, companies and the warning your own browser shows when a saved password has been compromised. We do not collect breaches ourselves.

That registry gathers the data already circulating after each theft, verifies it and indexes it so anyone can check whether their address is in there. The data is used under its Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence, which requires naming the source, which is why you will see it credited here and under every result.

What we add is not the data but the reading of it: what your particular result means, what to change first and why, written for someone who does not work in security.

My email is in a breach. What does that actually mean?

It means a company you once signed up with was robbed of its data, and your address was in the batch. It does not mean anyone has your current password or that your mailbox has been broken into. What matters is what leaked alongside your address.

Your address on its own getting out is annoying and little more. Your address together with a password is a different matter, especially if you reused that password somewhere else. That is why the result tells you what kind of data turned up in each case, not just how many times.

Do I have to change all my passwords?

No, and doing so usually backfires. Changing twenty passwords in one afternoon leads to picking twenty variations of the same one. Start with your email, move on to anything with money attached, and deal with any site where you reused a password.

The order matters more than the number, and it is laid out in what to do if your email was leaked.

Do I need an account?

Not for the free check. An account is only needed if you buy the action plan, because the access has to belong to someone.

If you do create one, what we keep is your email address and an encrypted, irreversible version of your password, never the password itself.

What does the action plan include?

A plan written for your specific result, ordered by what to deal with first, in plain language. You read it on the site and can download it as a PDF to print or keep. Access lasts 30 days, and you can come back and update it during that time.

What it is not: it is not antivirus, it does not erase you from the internet and it does not recover a lost account. It is the list of what to change, in what order, and why.
